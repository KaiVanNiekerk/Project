NOTE_ON_VALIDATION

Summary:
The codebase is currently optimizing a likelihood-based objective, not a CRPS-based objective. In the K2VAE path, training loss is built from the ELBO / negative log-likelihood (NLL) term, plus KL and reconstruction terms; in the baseline PatchTST path, evaluation computes CRPS separately as a test-time metric, but the training objective is still NLL-based. This means the raw loss curves are expected to be dominated by NLL, especially when the sigma (uncertainty) term is poorly calibrated.

Why NLL dominates training curves:
- NLL is extremely sensitive to uncertainty calibration, not just point accuracy.
- If sigma is underestimated, the log-likelihood becomes very large (negative log probability) even when the mean forecast is reasonable.
- The NLL term therefore often dwarfs the scale of rec or KL in the plotted loss curves.
- This is a property of the objective, not necessarily evidence that the model is failing at forecasting.

CRPS implementation:
The CRPS function in src/metrics.py is the standard empirical CRPS identity:
    CRPS(F_n, x) = E|X - x| - 0.5 * E|X - X'|
This is mathematically valid for empirical predictive samples and is used as a proper scoring rule for probabilistic forecasting evaluation.

Important caveat:
- CRPS is computed from sampled predictive draws and represents a distributional quality metric.
- It is not the same as the training objective being optimized.
- So a model can have a large NLL loss while still producing competitive CRPS / NMAE values.

Why tuning does not remove the effect completely:
- Hyperparameter tuning changes the optimization trade-offs, but it does not change the fact that the training objective is likelihood-based.
- If the model remains miscalibrated or overconfident, the NLL term will still dominate.
- This means the loss curve should be interpreted as an optimization signal, not as a direct measure of downstream forecast quality.

Practical interpretation for comparison tables:
Use NMAE and CRPS as the primary comparison metrics for forecasting quality, because they are the metrics aligned with the paper's evaluation goals.
The loss curve can still be useful for diagnosing training stability and calibration, but it should not be read as a one-to-one indicator of final forecasting performance.

Conclusion:
The current setup is internally consistent: the project is optimizing NLL while evaluating CRPS and NMAE as reporting metrics. The NLL-heavy curves are therefore expected behavior under the objective, rather than an obvious calculation error.
